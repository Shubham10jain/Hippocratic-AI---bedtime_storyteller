# dino-ballet

## Pipeline (read-aloud script)

The Dinosaur Who Wanted to Pirouette

At the far edge of Fernwick Wood, where the ferns grew so tall they tickled the bellies of passing clouds, lived a brontosaurus called Mabel Quill. She had a neck like a crane, a tail like a ribbon of green silk, and feet so enormous that she was never quite sure where the ground was going to be.

Beneath a mossy root, in a burrow with a round front door, lived her friend Old Sorrel. He was a hedgehog who hiccupped every time he yawned. [pause] Which was often.

Every evening, Mabel watched the swallows swoop above the meadow. They dipped. They spun. They looped the loop and came out the other side looking very pleased with themselves. And Mabel decided, with her whole long tail, that she wanted to be a ballerina.

"I shall pirouette," she announced one evening.

"Hic," said Old Sorrel.

"A pirouette," said Mabel, "is a spin on one foot. I have four feet, so I am already halfway there."

Her first attempt took place on the lake, using a lily pad as a stage. Mabel rose onto her tiptoes, which for a brontosaurus means lifting roughly one small tree. She leapt. She landed.

[sound: splash]

The water went up like a firework and came down on a family of ducks, who said something rude in duck and waddled off in a huff. Old Sorrel, who had been watching from the bank, gave an enormous yawn, hiccupped three times, and tumbled straight into a puddle.

"Lily pads," said Mabel, dripping, "are not built for ballerinas. They are built for frogs."

Her second attempt was more carefully planned. She fashioned a tutu from an old tablecloth, tied it around her middle with a bit of vine, and climbed onto a tree stump at the edge of the meadow.

"Balance," she told herself, "is simply falling very politely in the right direction."

She lifted one foot. She lifted the other. She began to turn, slowly at first, then faster, then faster still, until the tablecloth whirled and the grass hissed and the stump went wobbling beneath her. Mabel did not stop. She could not stop. She spun right off the stump and across the meadow in a blur of checks and fringe, and she came to rest, with a crash, on top of a picnic.

[pause]

There was a jam sandwich under one knee and a teapot under the other. A bun had landed squarely on the end of her nose, where it stayed.

Mabel sat up in the grass, crumbs on her chin, and sighed.

It was then that she noticed something. The swallows were not spinning all at once. Each one, before it dipped, tilted its head towards the breeze and held very still. It seemed to be listening. Only then did it lean, and only then did it turn, and each swallow turned in its own time, as though the wind had told it when to begin.

"They're not copying each other at all," Mabel whispered. "They're listening."

She thought about her lily pad, and her stump, and the ducks, and the teapot. She thought about how much noise she had made, and how little she had listened. A ballet, she realised, could not be a copy of the birds. It had to be slow, and careful, and her own.

So the next evening Mabel did not jump. She did not spin. She stood at the edge of the meadow, closed her eyes, and waited for the breeze to come.

When it came, it rustled the ferns, and Mabel swayed with it, gently, the way a reed sways or a tall tree leans. She lifted one foot, tiptoed, and set it down again. She wobbled a little. She let the wobble be.

Old Sorrel, who had come to watch with a leaf on his head, went very still. Then the ducks, who had returned to see what the splashing was about, began to bob their heads in time.

Mabel called her dance the Slow Sway. It was made of wobbles and tiptoes and long, patient breaths, and it had one special rhyme she sang under her breath while she danced:

[together: Tippy-toe, and gently go.]

The ducks loved it. The frogs in the lake, who had never before seen a brontosaurus dance, climbed onto their lily pads to watch, and nobody sank them.

When the moon rose over the ferns and the meadow turned silver, Mabel finally stopped. She folded her long neck and lowered herself, carefully, beside her friend's burrow. Old Sorrel curled into a small brown ball against her side.

[yawn]

"Hic," he murmured, and fell asleep.

Mabel began to hum, very softly, a tune with no steps in it at all. The wind dropped to a whisper. The ferns stopped their tickling. One by one, the swallows folded their wings and tucked themselves into the eaves, and the wood grew quiet, and Mabel Quill, who wanted to dance, drifted off to sleep still swaying, just a little, with the breeze.

## Baseline (original single call)

**Tyrone the Tyrannosaurus Tutu**

Tyrone was no ordinary Tyrannosaurus rex. While his cousins spent their days stomping through the swamp and roaring at Triceratops, Tyrone had a secret dream: he wanted to be a ballerina.

Every morning at dawn, he'd sneak off to a clearing by the waterfall, put on a pink tutu he had woven from ferns, and practice his pliés. The problem was that his arms were the size of tree stumps, and his tiny front claws couldn't quite reach his own head for the proper arm positions.

"Graceful as a swan," he'd whisper to himself, then promptly crash into a palm tree.

One day, the Great Dinosaur Dance Festival was announced. The grand prize was a golden tiara and a solo performance at the Jurassic Opera House. Tyrone's heart leapt. He signed up immediately, which caused his mother, Big Mama Rexina, to nearly swallow her breakfast whole.

"Tyrone, sweetheart," she said, "you're a *Tyrannosaurus rex*. Your ancestors hunted things. You can't go around doing... *that*."

"That" was a grand jeté, which Tyrone performed by leaping across the river. He landed in the middle, sending a tidal wave of mud onto the entire Stegosaurus family.

"Mama, I just want to dance!" he wailed.

His Uncle Gnash, the toughest T. rex in the valley, grumbled from behind a fallen log. "Dancing? Pfft. Real dinosaurs don't dance."

Then Gnash tried to do a pirouette to prove a point. He spun twice, wobbled, and fell straight into the mud, rolling like a giant tumbleweed before landing flat on his back with a loud *THUD* that shook three nearby volcanoes.

There was a long silence. Then Tyrone giggled. Then Mama giggled. Soon the whole valley was laughing.

"Alright," Gnash groaned, pulling a fern out of his teeth. "Teach me that twirl thing, kid."

So on the morning of the festival, Tyrone took the stage in his shimmering tutu, with Uncle Gnash lurking in the wings wearing a sparkly leotard he'd borrowed from a Pteranodon. The crowd went silent as Tyrone rose onto his tiptoes, arms raised as gracefully as a T. rex can manage.

Then he leapt, spun, and landed perfectly... right on the judges' table, which collapsed into a thousand pieces.

The crowd erupted. The judges, buried in splinters, held up their scorecards.

They all read: **10, 10, 10, and "Please come back next year and bring more chairs."**

Tyrone won the golden tiara. He wore it proudly for the rest of his life, which, since he was a dinosaur, was about 28 years. He never did master the perfect arabesque, but he did become the most beloved performer in the valley.

And every morning after that, the sun rose over the waterfall to the sound of thundering footsteps, tiny arms waving, and one very determined dinosaur whispering, "Graceful as a swan..."

*THUD.*
