# alice-bob

## Pipeline (read-aloud script)

Alice and Bob Under the Big Apple Tree

Alice lives in a small yellow house.
It has a red door.
Next to the house stands a tall apple tree.

Bob is Alice's best friend.
Bob is a gray cat.
He loves to sit in the sunny grass.
Alice sits there too.

[together: Bob and Alice, side by side.]

One morning, Bob's favorite ball rolled off.
It was blue, and it bumped under the fence.
[sound: plip]
Then it was gone.

[pause]

Bob sat very still under the tree.
His ears went flat.
He did not meow.
He did not move.

[softly] Alice saw his sad face.
"Don't worry, Bob," she said.
"I will help."

Alice found a big, long stick.
She pushed it under the fence.
"Come on, ball! Come on!"

[sound: crack]

The stick broke in two.
The ball did not come.
Alice frowned.
"Ugh!" she said.
She stomped away.
She did not look back.

[pause]

Alice sat on the front step.
She thought about Bob.
She thought about his flat ears.
Her cross feeling got smaller.
Her cross feeling got smaller and smaller.

[slowly] So Alice walked back.
She sat down beside Bob.
"Bob," she said softly, "what do you need?"

Bob looked up at her.
"Mrr," he said.
"I miss playing together."

[together: Bob and Alice, side by side.]

Alice smiled.
"Let's find your ball. Together."

They crawled under the gap in the hedge.
Leaves tickled their noses.
Bob went first. Alice went second.

[whisper] Under a green leaf, they found it.
The blue ball!
And right next to it sat a sleepy snail.

[pause]

The snail did not say a word.
It just blinked very slowly.

Alice giggled.
Bob laughed too.
[sound: chirp]

"I'm sorry I walked away, Bob," said Alice.
"I should have stayed with you."

Bob purred.
He rubbed his soft gray head against her hand.
Purr. Purr. Purr.

[together: Bob and Alice, side by side.]

[slowly] The sun slid down behind the apple tree.
The sky turned pink, then orange.
The grass grew soft and cool.

Alice and Bob walked home.
They walked slowly.
Alice held the blue ball.
She tucked it safely into her pocket.

[softly] One by one, the stars came out.

[yawn]

Bob and Alice, side by side.
Side by side.

[together: Bob and Alice, side by side.]

## Baseline (original single call)

**Alice and Bob**

Alice lived at the end of Maple Lane, in a yellow house with a crooked chimney and a garden that was always a little bit wild. She was nine years old, and she had exactly one best friend in the whole world.

His name was Bob, and he was a cat.

Bob was orange, the color of marmalade, with white socks on all four paws and a tail that curled up at the tip like a question mark. He had appeared on Alice's windowsill one rainy Tuesday, soaking wet and extremely dignified about it, and he had looked at her through the glass as if she were the one who had come to visit *him*.

She opened the window. He walked in, shook himself off over her bedroom rug, and said, "Hello. I'll be staying for dinner."

Alice had been too surprised to say anything. Then she had said, "Do you talk?"

"Only to people worth talking to," Bob had replied. "Mostly I just think very loudly and hope someone hears."

That was how it began.

---

Every morning after that, Bob waited on the garden wall while Alice walked to school. He never went with her, because he said schools were full of dogs pretending to be teachers, but he always walked her as far as the bakery on the corner. He would tell her things along the way: that the pigeons on Fifth Street were gossiping about the mayor, that the old man with the newspaper was secretly a retired pirate, that the best place in town for sunbeams was the hood of Mrs. Tanaka's blue car.

Alice believed almost all of it.

At school, she kept Bob a secret. She knew how it would sound. So at lunch she sat under the big oak tree and told her friend Priya about him instead, and Priya's eyes went very round.

"You have a *talking cat*?"

"He's not mine," Alice said. "He's my friend. There's a difference."

"What does he say?"

"Mostly he complains about the food," said Alice, "and he says I should stop eating my sandwich so fast."

Priya looked suspicious. Alice, to be fair, did not look like someone who was making things up.

---

The trouble started in October.

It was a windy afternoon, and Alice came home to find the back gate hanging open and the garden strangely silent. No birds. No rustle in the hedge. Bob was not on the wall.

She found him in the shed, curled on an old sack of birdseed, his orange fur puffed up and his eyes narrowed.

"Bob? What's wrong?"

"Nothing," said Bob. "Everything. I'm old, Alice."

"You're not old. You're a cat."

"I'm nine lives into my ninth life. I'm going to need to be honest with you about something." He paused. "I can't stay forever."

Alice sat down on the floor of the shed, right in the dust. "What does that mean?"

"It means cats don't live as long as people do, and I was never going to be an exception. I only got to talk to you because someone very old and very kind decided to lend me a voice for a while. And I think..." He stopped and cleared his throat, which was an odd thing for a cat to do. "I think the loan is almost up."

Alice didn't cry, not right away. She just reached out and put her hand on his back, the way she had on the very first day, and felt him breathe.

"Then we'll make the time count," she said.

Bob blinked slowly, which Alice had learned was his version of smiling.

---

So they did.

They climbed the big oak together and watched the whole town turn gold in the evening. They chased the pigeons off Fifth Street and then felt bad about it and bought them crumbs from the bakery. They visited the old man with the newspaper, who turned out to be a retired pirate after all, and who taught Alice to tie four different knots and Bob to hiss at seagulls in a way that made them fly off in alarm.

They talked. They talked about everything. Bob told her about the cats who had lived before him, and the dogs he had argued with, and the time he had once sat on a mayor. Alice told him about her dreams, about wanting to build bridges someday, about how frightening and wonderful it was to grow up.

"Will you remember me?" Bob asked one evening, as the streetlights came on.

"Every single day," said Alice.

"Good," said Bob. "Someone has to remember the sunbeams."

---

The next winter, on the first real snowfall, Alice went out to the garden wall and waited. Bob did not come.

She waited the next day, too, and the day after that.

She didn't hear his voice again. Not on the wall, not in the shed, not on the windowsill. But on the morning of the third day, when she walked to school, she found a single orange hair caught on the gate latch, and a patch of sun on the warm hood of Mrs. Tanaka's blue car, right where he had always liked to sit.

Alice smiled, and put her hand on the car, and kept walking.

Some friendships don't need words to last. But it helps, sometimes, to have had a cat who could say *I'll be staying for dinner*, and meant it.

---

*The End.*
